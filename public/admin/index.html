Plugin & bundle names — the naming landscape

A sampling of what each company actually calls its products, tagged by naming vibe. The pattern to notice: the most respected brands on this list cluster at two disciplined poles — functional (the name is the job) and playful (human wordplay) — and almost nobody leans "epic-fantasy." That's the lens for the Prism question below.

Vibe key Functional — name = the job Evocative / cinematic — mood over specs Playful / nostalgic — wordplay Descriptive — names what it's for Epic-fantasy / elemental Authentic / ethnographic

Prism's names — the split you're sensing is real, and it's fixable surgically

Prism doesn't have one naming style — it has two, and they pull in opposite directions. The World Instrument names are pitch-perfect. The FX/synth names are where the "fantasy-novel / gamer" read comes from. You don't need to rename the catalog — you need to bring the effects line into the same disciplined register the instruments already live in.

Authentic & credible — keep exactly as-is

  • World Instruments: Kora, Oud, Duduk, Koto, Sitar, Erhu, Bansuri, Hang Drum, Nyckelharpa, Hurdy Gurdy, Berimbau, Morin Khuur, Uilleann Pipes.
  • Real instrument names = instant authority and searchability. This is the naming a composer trusts. It's also better than most boutiques manage.

Epic-fantasy / elemental — the "cheese" risk

  • FX & synths: Dragonbreath, Void Noise Machine, Magma Hard Synth, Pyre Spring Reverb, Vortex Auto-Wah, Zephyr Flanger, Aether Ring Mod, Stellar Supersaw, Telluric Analog Synth.
  • Collections: Warpath, Tengri, Selva, Fuego, Fjord.
  • Reads as hobbyist exactly when an unknown two-person brand needs to read as trustworthy. It also buries what the plugin does under a theme.
The models to steer toward: the two most-admired naming systems here go opposite but equally disciplined directions — Baby Audio (playful-but-human: Spaced Out, Comeback Kid, Smooth Operator) and Valhalla / FabFilter (functional: VintageVerb, Pro-Q). Both make the name serve the product. Note Valhalla proves a mythic wrapper can work — but only as a light prefix on an otherwise plain, descriptive name (Valhalla Room), never as the whole identity. Pick one pole for Prism's FX line and commit; the instruments already show you can be disciplined. One more data point from the majors: the only big company naming in Prism's epic register is EastWest (Voices of the Empire, Ancient Kingdom, RA) — and they back it with an actual Hollywood studio, 38 years of history, and even then anchor their flagships on place-prestige (Hollywood Orchestra), not fantasy.

Proposed direction: name the FX line after light, not dragons

Prism already owns a stronger theme than fantasy: light. Refraction, spectrum, optics. It is scientific and sophisticated, it fits "Analog Soul. Digital Precision," and no competitor owns it. Audio effects map onto optical phenomena almost one to one, so the names read as clever rather than decorated, and they still form a collectible family the way Dragonbreath was reaching for. The world instruments (Kora, Oud, Duduk) do not change. This is the FX and synth side only.

Two backup directions

  • Functional (the FabFilter route): let the job be the name. Prism Delay, Prism Wah, Prism Drive, Prism Synth One. Boring on purpose. Best fit for the pedal line, where the names are already functional; there the only fix is the umbrella (retire Dragonbreath, call it the Circuit Series or just Prism Pedals).
  • Playful-human (hold for later): Baby Audio's register works for beatmakers but sits oddly next to Kora on a composer page. Keep it in the drawer for a future free or budget line.

Rename regardless of theme (legal + culture)

  • Warpath is the catalog's riskiest name: a war theme over Indigenous and ceremonial instruments invites exactly the cultural criticism to avoid. Use Ceremony or Rites (the bundle copy already says "War Horns & Ceremony").
  • Astral Rhodes and Ether Wurlitzer use live, defended trademarks. Convention: Tine EP (Rhodes uses tines) and Reed EP (Wurlitzers use reeds). Comet Juno should become something like Poly 8.
  • Culturally rooted collection names (Griot, Raga, Ceol, Tengri) are fine. Keep them.

Trademark register check (US / EU / UK)

A search of the public registers (USPTO via TSDR, EU and UK via EUIPO's TMview aggregator), focused on Class 9 (software) and Class 15 (musical instruments). This is a factual read of what is registered, not legal advice, and it does not replace a trademark attorney. Class 9 is enormous, so nearly every real word has some registration in it. What matters is whether a collision sits in your goods (audio and music software) or an unrelated lane.

Headline: the real exposure is the word "Prism," not the product names. "Prism Audio Labs" as a full name is unregistered everywhere. But the distinctive element, "Prism," is contested in audio Class 9 by established owners: Prism Sound (US "PRISMSOUND", Reg 2195242, professional audio equipment) and Headstock Distribution (UK "PRISM", filed Nov 2025, covering mixers, amps, mics, loudspeakers), plus a live EU "Prism High Definition Sound," and unregistered plugin uses (Tokyo Dawn's TDR Prism, IK's Prism Reverb, a Jackson Audio Prism pedal). Audio hardware and audio software are related goods in one class, and "Audio Labs" is descriptive, so this is a genuine potential obstacle for the brand, and every "Prism [Word]" product inherits it. Put this in front of a trademark attorney before investing further in the name.

Strategy read for Prism

You asked directly: does "our plugins work together + we're building world instruments" put you ahead — or should you think differently, given the goal is revenue as fast as possible? Here's the honest read, then a sequenced plan that plays to your two-person team (a career audio engineer/producer-turned-sales-exec + a web/comms/marketing lead — genuinely well-matched to go-to-market).

Edge: MODERATE

“Our plugins work together”

Interoperability is real and likable, but it's a retention / lifetime-value lever, not an acquisition hook. It rewards people who already own several of your plugins — it rarely drives the first purchase, which is what fast revenue needs. Heavyocity (shared engine), FabFilter (unified UX), and Baby Audio (a subscription that unifies its catalog) all have "works together" stories, so the claim isn't ownable on its own. Prism Link is more technically ambitious than their bundling — so keep it, but frame it as the reason to buy the bundle / come back, not the headline that wins a cold visitor.

Edge: STRONG (but niche)

“We make world instruments”

This is your real wedge. Physically-modeled world instruments — kora, oud, koto, duduk, hurdy-gurdy — with no Kontakt dependency and tiny file sizes is a position almost nobody owns at breadth. It's defensible and memorable. Two caveats: the world-instrument market is smaller than mixing FX, and cultural authenticity is a credibility (and PR) risk. De-risk it by collaborating with and crediting real players/experts — which doubles as your best marketing.

What the majors change (and don't)

Adding Native Instruments, Waves, Soundtoys, EastWest, Spectrasonics and Universal Audio clarifies the map rather than crowding it. The majors compete on catalog breadth, licensed authority, ecosystem lock-in, and discount treadmills — and each strength opens a gap a boutique can take: their perma-sales and subscription pushes have burned ownership trust (Waves' forced-subscription attempt collapsed in 3 days; NI hit insolvency in Jan 2026 and was sold to inMusic), their per-product character is shallow, and their "weirdness budget" is near zero. Three specifics for Prism: EastWest's RA/Silk are the incumbent world-instrument products — heavyweight sample banks in a proprietary player, so Prism's counter is modeled playability, tiny footprints and real-player provenance, not breadth. Spectrasonics is the blueprint — a husband-and-wife shop, four never-discounted products, satellites that plug into a flagship engine (what Prism Link should become). And Soundtoys proves the scale works — ~16 people, 23 opinionated effects, near-default status.

The bigger issue than either advantage: focus

The single biggest threat to "revenue fast" isn't your competitors — it's launching 79 plugins from a two-person, unproven team. It reads as thin, invites skepticism, and spreads your demos, support, and marketing impossibly thin. Every company on this list that grew fast started narrow: Valhalla owned "reverb," FabFilter owned "the EQ," Baby Audio owned "a vibe," Vynl launched with two plugins and a viral tone. Breadth is a moat you earn after revenue, not the thing that generates it.

Sequenced plan for fastest revenue

Ordered. Priority tags: DO NOW pre/at launch · NEXT first 90 days · LATER once revenue is flowing.

Launch ONE hero collection, not the whole universe DO NOW

Pick your single most differentiated, most demo-able collection — most likely a flagship world-instrument set (that's your ownable story) or the Dragonbreath FX line (easier for producers to "get"). Ship it superbly with 6–10 killer audio demos. Keep the other 70 plugins as a visible "roadmap" that builds anticipation and an email list.

Focus is the highest-leverage move on this page. It fixes credibility, demos, and support load at once.

Lead with sound — audio/video demos everywhere DO NOW

Nothing sells an audio plugin like audio. Inline "Play demo" on every product (like Spitfire, Teletone, Heavyocity, Westwood), plus 1–2 short "in-context" videos of the instruments in a real track. This is the #1 gap on your current site and the #1 conversion driver on every strong competitor.

Your husband can produce reference tracks that show the instruments off — a rare in-house advantage.

Give away one free "taster" plugin DO NOW

Valhalla's Supermassive, Spitfire LABS, and Goodhertz freebies are acquisition engines: they build email lists, word-of-mouth and trust at near-zero cost. Release one free world instrument or a lite FX unit as your top-of-funnel and gate future launches behind that list.

Fastest, cheapest way to build the audience you'll sell the paid launch to.

Weaponize your founders' story and network DO NOW

Two assets most boutiques lack: a career audio engineer/producer turned tech-sales exec, and a marketing/comms lead. Put your faces and story on the site (trust!), and have him do outbound the way sales execs do — line up artist/composer endorsements, sync/library/game-audio relationships, and education deals before launch. Have her run the launch calendar, PR, and content.

This team is unusually good at go-to-market. Most competitors here are engineers who can't sell. Lean into it hard.

Fix trust + support IA in the redesign DO NOW

Real Docs / FAQ / System Requirements / Contact pages (not links to /about), visible reviews as they come in, a press/endorsement strip, and resolved factual inconsistencies (74 vs 79). Low effort, high credibility return for an unknown brand asking for money.

Frame Prism Link as the bundle/upsell reason NEXT

Keep interoperability — but sell it as "buy the collection and they modulate each other" and "your library grows into a system," i.e., a driver of bigger carts and repeat purchase, not the cold-open pitch. Bundles are how every catalog on this list raises order value.

Add fast, high-margin revenue lines NEXT

Your MIDI pattern library (10k patterns) and preset packs are low-effort, high-margin, and can ship before the big instruments to start cash flow and prove demand. Given your husband's engineering chops, bespoke/custom sound-design work (à la SonalSystem) can fund the company early while the catalog builds.

Revenue this quarter, not next year — directly serves the "as fast as possible" goal.

Keep perpetual + bundles now; consider recurring later LATER

Your anti-subscription, transparent pricing is a genuine differentiator and lowers first-purchase friction — right for launch. Once you have a catalog and a base, a rent-to-own or "all-access" plan (Baby Audio, Output, Black Salt models) can add predictable recurring revenue without abandoning perpetual buyers.

Build a content/SEO surface LATER

Heavyocity, Spitfire, and Baby Audio all run editorial/tutorial content that compounds discovery. A blog on world-instrument technique, scoring, and "how it's modeled" plays perfectly to your niche and your marketing lead — a durable, cheap acquisition channel once the launch dust settles.

Bottom line

World instruments = yes, that's your lead. It's the differentiated, ownable, defensible story — pursue it, but focus it and de-risk the cultural authenticity with real players. Interoperability = keep it, demote it to an upsell/retention message. And the fastest revenue lever isn't a new feature at all — it's narrowing the launch, proving the sound with demos, and pointing your unusually strong sales+marketing team at a specific, reachable audience (composers, sync/game audio, world-music producers) rather than trying to be everything to everyone on day one.
